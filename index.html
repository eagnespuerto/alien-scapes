How this works

Every colour on screen is calculated: a spectrum integrated against the response of the human eye. None of them were picked by hand.

Light

The star is treated as a blackbody at its effective temperature. Planck's law gives the emitted spectrum; an atmospheric transmission profile attenuates it along a slant path whose length follows from the star's elevation, exactly as Beer's law requires. The surviving spectrum is integrated against the CIE 1931 2° standard observer to get tristimulus XYZ, then converted to sRGB through the D65 primaries. The code is ported from starlight-filter, which uses it to drive a display's gamma ramp.

Sky, ground and water

A Rayleigh atmosphere scatters short wavelengths out of the beam, so the sky is lit by what the beam lost. The same model gives a blue noon and a red sunset without either being coded in. Dust and haze absorb instead of scattering, so those skies take the transmitted tint rather than its complement. That is why Mars is butterscotch. Ground colour is the downwelling light times a mineral reflectance curve, integrated the same way.

Vegetation

Photosynthesis is a quantum process, so the model counts photons rather than energy. A reaction-centre pigment is chosen by weighing how many photons it can capture against how much energy each one is worth once thermalised down to the trap. Without that second term, far-red pigments would win under every star. Antenna pigments are then layered on, subject to what can physically attach to what. The leaf's reflectance is what survives that absorption, and its colour is the illuminant times that reflectance.

Limits of the model

Pigment absorption curves are sums of Gaussians placed at literature peak positions. The peaks are real; the band shapes are modelled, not digitised from measured spectra. Stellar luminosities and radii are rounded literature values. Habitable-zone bounds use the Kopparapu et al. (2013) coefficients, which are fitted for 2600–7200 K and are flagged as extrapolated outside that range. The nitroplast is a nitrogen-fixing organelle that has lost photosystem II and RuBisCO, so it does not fix carbon. Its curve models the photosystem I antenna it still carries.

This is a worldbuilding and teaching tool, not a radiative-transfer code.
